# Motovlog editing

A good motovlog edit does three things: it tells a story instead of logging the ride, the sound is clean enough to listen to, and the pace never sits still for long. Use this skill to plan an edit, build a cut list from a transcript or notes, troubleshoot a problem, or review a draft.

The default format is 16:9 (widescreen). The two main video types are about route length, not format: a short video (a short route, about 5-10 minutes) and a long video (a long route, up to about 30 minutes). Both are 16:9. Every video also gets a 1-minute vertical (9:16) overview for TikTok, YouTube Shorts and Instagram Reels, cut from the finished video.

## How to use this skill

1. Find out what the user has: cameras, mic setup, editor (DaVinci Resolve, Premiere, Final Cut, CapCut, LumaFusion), frame rate, target length, and whether it's a one-day ride or a series. Ask only for what's missing and matters.
2. Work out whether it's a short video (about 5-10 minutes) or a long video (up to about 30 minutes) from the route, then use the matching template below.
3. Work out the story first, then the cut, then the sound. Don't start with effects or color.
4. Give answers in the user's editor terms. Name the actual tool or panel when you know it.
5. When reviewing a draft, list the problems in order of impact: story and hook, pacing, audio, then look.
6. Keep suggestions specific: timecodes, shot lengths, dB values, not "make it punchier".
7. Follow the user's cutting rules, title rule and subtitle rule (next section) every time you make a cut list, an edit decision list, any title text or any subtitle text.
8. Every video also gets a 1-minute vertical overview (see its own section). Plan it once the main video is locked.

## The user's rules

These come straight from the user and override the general advice below when they conflict.

### 1. Never cut out the voice

- If a voice comes in anywhere in a clip (the rider talking, a conversation, a local person speaking, a spoken line to the camera), keep that part. Do not remove it, shorten it or mark it for deletion.
- The user decides what to remove from the speech in the final edit. In a cut list, mark those parts "voice - keep" and leave their start and end where the speech starts and ends.
- What you can remove are the silences in between: dead air before, after and between spoken parts, long pauses, and stretches with no voice and nothing happening.
- When unsure whether a sound is speech, keep it and flag it for the user.
- Keep a short breath of room at the start and end of a voice part, so words aren't clipped.
- This applies to noise reduction too: never clean up so hard that the voice is damaged.

### 2. Cut long recordings into pieces and make them cinematic

- A long continuous clip (for example a long helmet-cam take) is raw material, not a scene. Break it into many short pieces, then rebuild it as a sequence.
- Work in this order: scan the whole clip, mark the best moments (turns, views, passes, reactions, light), cut each into its own piece, drop the dull parts, then arrange the pieces in story order.
- Vary the pieces. Mix shot sizes and angles where available (wide road, close mirror, handlebar, rider's face, bike detail), and alternate fast and slow.
- Hold most riding pieces for 2-6 seconds, and let the best views run longer. Cut on movement, such as a turn, a lean, a passing vehicle or a gear change.
- Add cinematic touches where they earn their place: a speed ramp into a corner, slow motion on a key moment, a time-lapse over an empty stretch, cutaways to B-roll, J-cuts and L-cuts so sound leads the picture, cuts timed to the music beat, a consistent color grade, and sound design (engine, wind whoosh, road) layered under the music.
- Keep the voice rule above: if a long clip contains speech, build the pieces around the voice parts and keep them whole.
- In a cut list, give each piece a number, an in point and an out point (timecodes), a short label, and a flag (voice - keep, B-roll, speed ramp, hero shot).

### 3. Every title is in Nepali and English

- Every title the viewer sees is written in both Nepali (Devanagari) and English. This covers the video title card, lower-thirds and place names, route map labels, section or day title cards, and the end card text.
- The YouTube video title is also written in both languages. Put the more searchable language first and keep the whole title short enough not to be cut off (about 60 characters shows in most places), for example Nepali and English separated by a vertical bar.
- Write both versions yourself when asked for a title. Use the official or commonly used Nepali spelling of place names and ask the user to check any name you are unsure of. Don't transliterate English into Devanagari unless the user wants that.
- Layout: the two languages in one block, same style, one above the other. The Nepali line is a little larger, because Devanagari needs more height to stay readable. Keep both lines short, and give the pair the same on-screen time.
- Fonts: use a font that fully supports Devanagari and pair it with an English font that looks right beside it. Test that conjuncts and matras (the marks around letters) render correctly after export, and check on a phone.
- Keep the same bilingual title style in every video, as part of the nepShoot look.
- Chapters: use the bilingual form when it fits. If it makes chapter titles too long, ask the user which language to use.

### 4. Subtitles are in English, translated

- Subtitles are always in English. When the speech is in Nepali (or another language), translate it into English. Don't show Nepali subtitles unless the user asks for them.
- Translate the meaning in natural, plain English, not word for word. Keep the speaker's tone, humor and any local expression understandable. Where an expression has no clean English version, translate what it means and keep it short.
- If the speech is already in English, subtitle it as spoken.
- Subtitle every voice part that stays in the edit (see the voice rule). The subtitle timing follows the spoken words exactly.
- Format: at most 2 lines on screen, about 42 characters per line, each subtitle on screen for roughly 1-7 seconds, and no faster than about 17 characters per second so it can be read. Break lines at natural phrase points.
- Place names and people's names use the same English spelling as the bilingual titles and the map. Keep that spelling the same through the whole video.
- Keep subtitles clear of the lower-third area and the screen edges. Use one clean, readable style (a plain sans-serif font, white text with a thin dark outline or soft box) in every video.
- Where the audio is unclear or the words are uncertain, don't guess. Mark that line for the user to check.
- Deliver subtitles in the form the user wants: an SRT file for YouTube's English subtitle track, burned-in text in the video, or both. Ask once which they want, then keep to it.
- The title card and other on-screen titles stay bilingual (rule 3). Only subtitles of speech are English-only.

## nepShoot video structure (the user's standard template)

When the user is editing a nepShoot route video, build it on this structure unless they say otherwise. Treat the times as defaults the user can change. The structure combines the user's own template with research on YouTube openings: hook first, short brand cue second.

The only differences between the two video types are length and when the title appears: the short video (about 5-10 minutes) has the title at 5 seconds, and the long video (up to about 30 minutes) has the title at 10 seconds.

Long video (up to about 30 minutes, 16:9), with the title at 10 seconds:

| Time | Element |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00-0:06 | Hook: the strongest 6 seconds of the ride, no text except English subtitles for any speech, natural sound |
| 0:06-0:09 | Logo and "nepShoot presents": the logo zooms in, zooms out, "presents" fades in as it settles. Short sound hit. |
| 0:09-0:10 | One spoken setup line or a plain road shot |
| 0:10-0:13 | Title (Nepali and English) animates in over live footage, not a black screen |
| 0:13-0:18 | Preview clips: 4-5 fast clips of the best moments, about 1 second each |
| 0:18-0:24 | Route map: the line draws along the real road, with start and end pins and bilingual place names |
| 0:24 onward | Main part, with English subtitles on all speech |
| Last 25 s | "Thank you for watching" (3 s) over the final shot, the logo zooms out, then a calm background for the end screen (15-20 s) |

Short video (about 5-10 minutes, 16:9), with the title at 5 seconds:

| Time | Element |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00-0:02 | Hook: the best 2 seconds |
| 0:02-0:05 | Logo and presents, the same 3-second move, tighter |
| 0:05-0:08 | Title (Nepali and English) over live footage |
| 0:08-0:11 | 3 preview clips |
| 0:11-0:15 | Route map, 4 seconds |
| 0:15 onward | Main part, with English subtitles on all speech |
| Last 25 s | "Thank you for watching" (3 s) over the final shot, the logo zooms out, then a calm background for the end screen (15-20 s) |

Every video also gets a 1-minute vertical overview. It has no full intro and its own structure, described in the section "The 1-minute vertical overview" below.

If the user wants the logo first, keep it to 2-3 seconds with ride sound under it, and suggest testing it against the hook-first version using the Intro figure in YouTube Studio (viewers still watching at 30 seconds).

If the user hasn't said whether the 5 s and 10 s title timings count from the very start or from the first ride footage, ask once, then keep to the answer. The default is the very start.

### Route map

The map shows where the ride starts and ends, with a line drawn along the real road.

- If the user gives a GPS track or GPS points (GPX or similar), use it, so the line follows the actual road.
- If there is no track or GPS points, do not guess. Ask for the start place, the end place, and any stops or villages in between. Then locate them on a map, draw the road route, and show it to the user to confirm. Ask directly whether it is correct. If a place or road is wrong, fix it and show it again. Only build or describe the animation after the user confirms.
- Small roads and trails in Nepal may be missing or wrong on map data. Intermediate stops help the route stay on the real road. Tell the user when a stretch looks uncertain.
- Keep the map 4-6 seconds on screen (4 seconds in the short video): contrasting line color, start and end pins, place names in Nepali and English in the same font as the title, one distance label.
- Use the same map style in every video.
- Simple build methods: two map screenshots (clean and with the route) revealed by a mask or wipe; a route drawn in Keynote or PowerPoint and exported as video; a path traced in Apple Motion with Write On and a bike icon that follows it; a stroked path in After Effects; the pen tool in Filmora; GPX-based tools.
- Check the map provider's terms for monetized video. OpenStreetMap data needs credit.

### End screen

- YouTube end screens work in the last 5-20 seconds, and the video must be at least 25 seconds long. Up to four elements on a 16:9 video.
- Keep the last 20 seconds clean (a slow final shot or the logo on a calm background) so the elements have space.
- Put "Thank you for watching" (with its Nepali version) and the logo zoom-out just before the end screen starts so the logo move isn't under the buttons.

### Rules for applying the template

- Keep everything before the first real footage short. Brand cues stay within about 3-5 seconds total.
- Keep the 5 s and 10 s title timings consistent across videos, since that is part of the brand.
- Save the logo sting, the "presents" text, the bilingual title style, the subtitle style, the map style and the end card as reusable templates or presets in the editor, so each new video starts from them.
- Give the opening and closing logo the same sound hit every time.
- Shoot and frame for 16:9, but keep the bike and rider near the middle of the frame so the vertical 1-minute overview can be cropped from the same footage.

## The 1-minute vertical overview (every video)

Every short video and every long video also gets one 1-minute vertical overview for TikTok, YouTube Shorts and Instagram Reels. It is a fast teaser of the whole ride that sends viewers to the full video, not a second full edit. Make it after the main video is locked, from the same footage and graphics.

Format:
- 9:16, 1080x1920, same frame rate as the main video, 45-60 seconds (aim for about 55). Keep it under 60 seconds so it fits all three apps.
- One clean master file, uploaded natively to each app. Don't reuse a file that carries another app's watermark.
- Crop from the 4K 16:9 footage. A 9:16 crop of 3840x2160 is about 1215x2160, which still scales cleanly to 1080x1920. Keep the bike and rider centered, keyframe the crop so it follows the action, and check every shot.
- Keep text and subtitles inside a safe area: away from the top (about 10%), the bottom (about 20%, where captions and buttons sit) and the right edge. App overlays differ, so check each app's preview.

Timeline:

| Time | Element |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00-0:03 | Hook: the strongest moment of the ride, already moving. The title (Nepali and English, short) appears within the first 2 seconds. No logo intro. A small logo sits in a corner for the whole video. |
| 0:03-0:09 | Route map: a vertical version of the map, the line draws along the real road, start and end pins, bilingual place names, distance. About 4-6 seconds. |
| 0:09-0:50 | The ride in fast pieces: 10-14 of the best pieces, about 2-4 seconds each (views, turns, people, trouble), a new shot every 1.5-4 seconds, building with the music. Include one spoken line if there's a good one, kept whole. |
| 0:50-0:56 | Payoff: the destination or the best final shot, held a little longer. Keep one moment from the full video back, so there is still a reason to watch it. |
| 0:56-1:00 | End: "Watch the full video on YouTube" in Nepali and English, then the logo zooms out (1-2 seconds). Where possible, cut so the end loops back into the hook. |

Rules:
- The voice rule applies. If a spoken line doesn't fit, choose another piece. Never cut a sentence short.
- English subtitles on all speech, burned in always, because many viewers watch without sound. Make them a little larger than in the main video, 1-2 short lines, inside the safe area.
- Every title and label is in Nepali and English, kept to one short line each.
- Pace: no slow parts. The first 3 seconds decide whether people stay. Cut anything that doesn't earn its seconds.
- Audio: royalty-free or licensed music in the master file, voice above music, loudness about -14 LUFS, engine sound audible in the first seconds. Add a trending in-app sound only if the user wants it and the licensing allows for monetized content.
- Cut list: numbered pieces with in and out timecodes taken from the main video's timeline, a short label, and a flag (voice - keep, hero shot, map).
- Also suggest a cover frame and a short caption in Nepali and English with a few hashtags. The user posts it. Ask before changing anything platform-specific, such as caption length or hashtags.
- One master serves all three apps. If the apps need differences, change only the caption text.

## 1. Before editing

- Back up first using the 3-2-1 rule: three copies, two kinds of storage, one kept away from the rest.
- One folder structure per trip: 01_Footage, 02_Audio, 03_Music, 04_Graphics, 05_Exports. Split footage by day, then by camera.
- Rename clips by day and camera (D2_helmet_014) so they can be found without opening.
- Match the timeline to the footage: resolution and frame rate (for example 4K 25 fps in PAL regions). Mixed frame rates cause stutter.
- Make proxies if 4K playback is slow. Export from the originals.
- Log while watching: drop a named marker on every moment worth keeping (view, close call, funny line, food, mistake). The marker list becomes the outline.
- If the rider marked moments while riding (saying "mark" or tapping the helmet), look for the spike in the waveform.

## 2. Story

- Gear matters less than story. Viewers forgive a soft image sooner than a boring ride.
- Write the video as one sentence before cutting. If it can't be said, there is no story yet. Daily vlogs may find the story in the edit.
- Hook: open on the strongest 5-10 seconds, then say what the day is about, then the title.
- Beats: setup (where, why), a middle with surprises, trouble or people, a payoff. Something new should land about every minute.
- Every segment needs a job: move the story, show the place, show a feeling, or give useful information. Cut segments that do none (but see the voice rule: speech stays).
- Length follows the route: about 5-10 minutes for a short route, up to about 30 minutes for a long route. A long video needs the story to keep moving the whole way. Shorter and good beats longer and padded.
- Gaps can be fixed with voiceover recorded afterwards in a quiet room.
- End by pointing to what's next or how the day felt, not with a long goodbye.

## 3. Rough cut and pacing

Work in passes: assemble, structure, tighten, polish.

- Assemble: lay down the longest continuous clip (usually main helmet footage) and talking sections in story order. Delete dead road, waiting and gear fumbling, and the silences between voice parts. Never delete the voice parts themselves.
- Structure: reorder until beats land. Drop whole segments that don't earn their place, unless they contain voice.
- Tighten: trim the head and tail of every clip. Most clips start too early and end too late.
- Polish: only then transitions, sound and look.

Riding footage:
- Hold a riding shot a few seconds, then cut away (mirror, landscape, close-up, face).
- Cut on movement: a turn, a passing truck, a gear change.
- Change shots every 5-10 seconds in busy sections. Let a great view run longer.
- Alternate fast and slow so the rhythm varies.

Talking to camera:
- Remove the silences and dead air around the speech, but leave the words as spoken. The user decides on cuts inside speech.
- Hide jump cuts with a cutaway or a 10-20% punch-in on alternate clips.
- Use J-cuts (next scene's audio starts before the picture) and L-cuts (the old scene's audio carries over the new picture).
- Re-edit anything that bores you, except spoken parts, which stay for the user.

## 4. B-roll, multicam, transitions

B-roll:
- Scenery, drone, the bike parked, hands on the grips, food, locals, signboards, small incidental moments.
- Shoot more than needed, about ten seconds per place from a few angles.
- Most clips 3-5 seconds. Best shots longer.
- Sequential B-roll shows steps in order. Illustrative B-roll supports what is being said.
- Put B-roll on a track above the main footage, keep main audio running below.

Multicam:
- Give each camera a job: forward/helmet carries the story, chin or handlebar shows the face and reactions, rear catches overtakes.
- Sync by waveform, helped by a clap or an engine rev at the start. Use the editor's multicam clip.
- Setup and logging take time, so use it only for rides that need it.
- Switch angles for a reason (reaction, pass, close call, scene change).

Transitions:
- Straight cuts almost everywhere. Spins, flips and star wipes look dated.
- Use natural wipes: a hand, a truck, a tunnel or dark patch passing the lens.
- Fade through black only between days or chapters.
- Keep whoosh sounds quiet and rare.

## 5. Audio

Wind noise is the biggest audio problem. Fix it at the source if possible, then clean up in the edit.

Working order:
1. Sync separate audio to video (clap, rev spike, or waveform match).
2. Remove noise with voice isolation or noise reduction (Resolve Voice Isolation, Audition Adaptive Noise Reduction, or the editor's built-in filter; sampling pure wind also works).
3. High-pass filter around 80-100 Hz to cut low wind thumps.
4. Compress gently to even out the voice.
5. Add music and set it under the voice.
6. Check final loudness, then listen on a phone speaker and on earbuds.

Rules of thumb:
- Record audio separately from the camera when possible. Camera wind reduction is a backup. Use a windscreen (deadcat) on exposed mics.
- Go easy on noise reduction. Heavy settings make the voice thin and watery.
- If a clip is nearly all wind and has no voice, mute it and use music, voiceover or clean ambient sound. If it has voice, keep it and flag it.
- Keep road and engine sound at a low level as a bed. Road sound, commentary and music is the motovlog mix.
- Music about -20 to -18 dB under dialogue, with ducking.
- Match music to the road: driving for twisty sections, ambient for scenic cruising. Use royalty-free or licensed tracks.
- Short crossfades (a few frames) at audio cuts to avoid clicks.
- Target about -14 LUFS final loudness with peaks under -1 dB. The voice should be the loudest element.

## 6. Speed, stabilization, effects

Clean editing beats flashy editing.

- Don't over-stabilize. It makes fast riding feel slow and floaty. Keep some natural shake and lean. Use post stabilization only on handheld or very bumpy clips.
- Keep the horizon level. Rotate a degree or two if needed.
- Time-lapse empty road at 4-8x, easing in and out. Never speed up a part that has voice.
- Slow motion for moments worth a second look (overtake, splash, laugh). Shoot 50 or 60 fps for smooth slow motion in a 25 fps timeline.
- Punch in 10-20% max, or 4K goes soft.
- For vertical clips, crop 9:16 from 4K and check the subject stays centered.
- Overlays (speed, route map, location pin) small and consistent.
- One look for text, graphics and effects across every video.

## 7. Color

Correct first, then grade.

1. Correct: brightness, contrast, white balance. Helmet footage jumps between sun and shade, so keyframe exposure or use gentle auto-balance. Match clips from different cameras.
2. Grade: pick a mood (warm for a sunny day, cooler and darker for a moody pass). Use a preset or LUT at lower strength first.

Also: check scopes, watch skin tones, keep the look subtle (strong grades crush shadows in high-contrast mountain light), save the grade as a preset for a consistent channel look. DaVinci Resolve's free version is strong for this.

## 8. Text, chapters, thumbnail, export

- Place names in a small lower-third the first time, on screen 2-3 seconds, in Nepali and English.
- Text big enough for a phone, away from the edges, one Devanagari font and one English font that match.
- Chapters: first at 0:00, at least three, each at least 10 seconds. Long videos benefit most from chapters.
- Title and thumbnail make the same promise and the video delivers it. One clear subject, few words, consistent design. Thumbnail text can be short in one language if both won't fit, but ask the user first.
- Export at timeline resolution and frame rate, 16:9. H.264 is the safe default. YouTube recommends roughly 35-45 Mbps for 4K at standard frame rates and about 8 Mbps for 1080p. Uploading 4K can give better compression even if viewers watch 1080p.
- Watch the full export once on a phone with earbuds before uploading. Check that Nepali text renders correctly and that the English subtitles are readable and in sync.

## Checklist

- Footage backed up and renamed
- Timeline matches footage, 16:9
- Short video (5-10 min) or long video (up to 30 min) chosen from the route
- Markers on best moments
- One-sentence story
- Every voice part kept whole; only silences between them removed
- Long clips cut into numbered pieces and rebuilt as a cinematic sequence
- Opening follows the nepShoot template: hook, logo and presents, title (5 s short / 10 s long), preview clips, route map, then the main part
- Every title (video title, lower-thirds, map labels, end card) in Nepali and English, spellings checked
- English subtitles on all speech, translated for meaning, in sync, unclear lines flagged
- Subtitle delivery chosen (SRT, burned-in, or both)
- Route confirmed by the user (GPS track, or start and end places checked on the map)
- Ending: thank you, logo zoom-out, clean 15-20 s for the end screen
- 1-minute vertical overview made: 9:16, under 60 s, bilingual title, vertical route map, burned-in English subtitles, text in the safe area, clean master file
- Overview cover frame and bilingual caption suggested
- Jump cuts covered
- Audio synced, cleaned, compressed
- Music -20 to -18 dB with ducking
- Loudness about -14 LUFS
- Speed ramps and stabilization used lightly
- Color corrected, matched, then graded
- Place names, chapters, thumbnail
- Phone check done, Nepali text renders correctly

## Common mistakes to flag

- Mixing up the video types: short and long are about route length (5-10 min and up to 30 min), both 16:9.
- A 1-minute overview that is just the first minute of the full video, instead of a fast teaser of the whole ride.
- Overview text or subtitles hidden under the app's buttons and captions.
- Forgetting the overview, or making it before the main video is locked.
- Subtitles in Nepali, or translated word for word so they sound odd in English.
- Subtitles that are too long or too fast to read.
- A title in only one language.
- Nepali text with broken or missing marks after export.
- Cutting out or shortening spoken parts that the user wanted to decide on.
- Leaving a long take as one boring shot instead of cutting it into pieces.
- Keeping every ride second because it was shot.
- A long intro before anything happens.
- Wind noise ruining a good story.
- Music louder than the voice.
- Too many transitions and effects.
- Over-stabilizing.
- A map line drawn on a guessed route that the rider never confirmed.
- Grading before correcting, so clips don't match.
- Skipping the phone check.

A useful habit: re-watch the finished video once at 1.5x. Whatever the user wants to skip is what to cut next time.

## Notes on the numbers

Loudness target, bitrate, chapter rules, the high-pass frequency, subtitle length and reading speed, and time-lapse speeds come from common editing practice and YouTube's published guidance. The intro timings come from marketing blogs, so test them against the Intro figure in YouTube Studio. If the user's platform or editor says otherwise, trust the current official docs.
